About

Why we built FHLS

At the lunar south pole, where Artemis crews will land, the Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° and circles the horizon once a month, so a crater rim a few hundred metres away can cut off sunlight for days. Earth wobbles by up to 6.7° and can sink behind the hills for two weeks, taking the direct radio link with it. Choosing where and when to land means knowing both.

Our idea came from architecture: a sun-path diagram with a shading mask shows when light reaches a window. FHLS does the same for the Moon. It traces the real skyline around any spot from NASA elevation data and follows the Sun and Earth across it for every hour of 2027 and 2028. Mission planners get numbers, teachers get a picture of why the poles are special, and anyone can see what a crew would see.

How it's built

NASA data to your browser

  1. DataLRO LOLA elevation · JPL SPICE ephemeris (DE440)
  2. EnginePython traces the skyline in 180 directions and the Sun and Earth for all 17,544 hours
  3. BrowserJavaScript combines them for any click in about 10 ms. No server

r = 0.99 against NASA's LOLA illumination map, over 400 random south polar points (0.8 points mean difference).

Limits

What the numbers don't say

Sunlight and a view of Earth are planning inputs, not proof that a site is safe. Slope, boulders, temperature, dust, power systems and mission rules matter too. Away from the poles the terrain is coarser, and the app says so.
