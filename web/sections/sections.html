About

Why we built FHLS

At the lunar south pole, where Artemis crews will land, the Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° above the horizon. It circles the sky once a month, so a crater rim a few hundred metres away can cut off the light for days. Earth wobbles by up to 6.7° and can drop behind the hills for two weeks, taking the direct radio link with it. To choose where and when to land, you need to know both.

The idea came from architecture. Architects draw a sun-path diagram with a shading mask, the outline of everything around a building, to see when light reaches a window. FHLS does the same for the Moon. It traces the real skyline around any spot from NASA elevation data, then follows the Sun and Earth across it for every hour of 2027 and 2028. Planners get numbers, teachers get a picture of why the poles are special, and anyone can stand where a crew would stand.

How it's built

From NASA data to your browser

  1. DataLRO LOLA elevation · JPL SPICE ephemeris (DE440)
  2. EnginePython traces each spot's skyline in 180 directions, and places the Sun and Earth for all 17,544 hours
  3. BrowserJavaScript combines the two for any click in about 10 ms. No server, works offline

r = 0.99 against NASA's own LOLA illumination map at 400 random south polar points, with a mean difference of 0.8 percentage points.

Limits

What the numbers don't say

Sunlight and a view of Earth are planning inputs, not proof that a site is safe. Slope, boulders, temperature, dust, hardware and mission rules all matter too. Seeing the Sun is not the same as having power, and seeing Earth is not the same as having a link. Away from the poles the terrain is coarser, and the app says so.
