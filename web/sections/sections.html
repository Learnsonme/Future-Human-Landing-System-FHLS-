About

Why we built this

NASA asked for a tool that lets mission planners, teachers and the public compare landing sites and dates by where the Sun and Earth sit above the horizon. At the lunar south pole that question is hard. The Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° and circles the sky once every 29.5 days, so a crater rim a few hundred metres away can block it for days. Earth wobbles by up to 6.7° each month and can drop behind the hills for about two weeks.

The idea came from architecture. Architects draw a sun-path diagram with a shading mask, the outline of everything around a building, to see when sunlight reaches a window. FHLS does the same on the Moon. It traces the real skyline around any spot from NASA elevation data, then follows the Sun and Earth across it for every hour of 2027 and 2028. Planners get numbers, teachers get a picture of why the poles are special, and anyone can see what an Artemis crew would see.

How it's built

From NASA data to your browser

  1. NASA dataLRO LOLA elevation maps · JPL SPICE ephemeris (DE440)
  2. EnginePython works out the skyline in 180 directions at every 2 km around the south pole (250 m at Artemis sites), and the Sun and Earth for all 17,544 hours
  3. Browsera small JavaScript library combines them for any click in about 10 ms. No server, works offline

r = 0.99 against NASA's own LOLA illumination map: 0.8 percentage points mean difference over 400 random south polar points, and the same 37 permanently shadowed points.

Limits

What the numbers don't say

Sunlight and a view of Earth are planning inputs, not proof that a site is safe or usable. Slope, boulders, temperature, dust, power systems and mission rules all matter too. Seeing the Sun does not guarantee power, and seeing Earth does not guarantee a radio link. Away from the poles the terrain is lower precision, and the app says so.
